What Is a Good ATS Score? (And How to Raise Yours)

Resume checkers, including VarHireBlaze, give you an "ATS score", usually out of 100. It's a useful signal, but only if you know what it measures and what to do with it.

What the score actually measures

There is no single official ATS score shared by every company. Each tool calculates its own estimate, usually from a mix of things:

  • Keyword match: how many important skills and terms from the job description appear in your resume.
  • Readability: whether the text can be extracted cleanly, with standard sections and no text trapped in images.
  • Completeness: contact details, dates, education and a skills section are present.
  • Impact: whether your bullet points show results, not just duties.

So treat the score as a checklist with a number on top, not a pass/fail verdict from employers.

What counts as "good"

  • Below 50: something basic is wrong. The file may not be readable, or the resume barely matches the role.
  • 50–70: readable, but missing important keywords or measurable results. This is the most common range.
  • 70–85: a strong, well-targeted resume. Worth sending.
  • 85+: excellent match. Chasing a higher number beyond this usually isn't worth the time.

A score of 100 is not the goal. A resume stuffed with every keyword reads badly to the human who opens it next.

Why your score changes from job to job

The same resume can score 58 for a "Data Analyst" posting and 76 for a "Business Analyst" posting. That's expected, because the score depends on the job description you compare against. This is why sending one generic resume everywhere underperforms. Small edits per application make a real difference.

Seven ways to raise your score honestly

  1. Paste the job description into the checker so it compares against the real role, not a generic one.
  2. Add the missing skills you actually have. If you used SQL in a project but never listed it, list it.
  3. Use the employer's wording. "Customer success" and "client support" might mean the same thing to you, but a search only matches the exact phrase.
  4. Add numbers. "Reduced load time by 40%" or "Handled 30+ customer queries a day".
  5. Switch to one column and standard headings if your design is complex.
  6. Put skills in context. Mention a tool inside a bullet point, not only in the skills list.
  7. Remove filler like "hard-working team player". It uses space without adding matches.
Don't do this: hiding keywords in white text. Modern parsers extract all text, and recruiters who notice it will reject you.
