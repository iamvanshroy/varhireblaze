What Is an ATS? How Resume Filters Actually Work

If you have applied for jobs online and never heard back, you have probably wondered whether a human even saw your resume. Often the first thing that reads it is software called an Applicant Tracking System, or ATS. Knowing what that software does (and what it doesn't do) is the quickest way to stop losing applications for avoidable reasons.

What an ATS is

An ATS is the database a company uses to manage hiring. When you click "Apply", your resume goes into it along with every other applicant's. Recruiters then use the ATS to search, sort and shortlist candidates instead of opening hundreds of PDFs one by one. Workday, Greenhouse, Lever, iCIMS, Zoho Recruit and Darwinbox are common examples.

What happens to your resume inside it

  1. Parsing. The system pulls the text out of your file and tries to split it into fields: name, contact details, work history, education and skills.
  2. Storing. Those fields are saved in a candidate profile. If parsing went wrong, the profile is wrong. Your job title might end up in the "company" field, or your skills might be missing entirely.
  3. Searching and ranking. Recruiters search for terms like "Python", "React" or "B2B sales". Some systems also score how closely a profile matches the job description.
  4. Human review. A recruiter reads the profiles that came up in the search or ranked highest.

The big myth: "the ATS rejects you automatically"

Most ATS software does not secretly delete your application. The real problem is quieter: your resume never appears in the recruiter's search. That can happen because the system couldn't read it, or because it doesn't contain the words the recruiter typed. Companies can also add knockout questions, such as "Are you willing to relocate?" or "Do you have a valid work permit?", and those can filter applicants automatically.

What an ATS struggles to read

  • Text inside images. A skills section designed in Canva and exported as a picture is invisible to it.
  • Tables and multiple columns. Some parsers read across columns line by line, mixing two sections into nonsense.
  • Headers and footers. Contact details placed there are sometimes skipped.
  • Creative section names. "My Journey" instead of "Experience" can confuse the parser.
  • Icons instead of words. A phone icon next to a number is fine, but a skill shown only as a logo is not.

How to make your resume ATS-friendly

  • Use a single-column layout with standard headings: Summary, Experience, Projects, Education, Skills.
  • Save it as a text-based PDF (exported from Word or Google Docs, not scanned).
  • Mirror the exact words from the job description where they are true for you. If the job says "data visualisation", write that rather than only "made charts".
  • Spell out abbreviations once, for example "Machine Learning (ML)".
  • Keep dates in a consistent format, like "Jun 2025 – Present".
Quick test: open your PDF, press Ctrl+A, copy everything and paste it into a plain text editor. If the text comes out jumbled, in the wrong order, or missing, an ATS will probably struggle with it too.

The bottom line

An ATS is a filing system with a search bar. Your goal is simple: make your resume easy for it to read, and make sure the words a recruiter would search for are actually in it.
